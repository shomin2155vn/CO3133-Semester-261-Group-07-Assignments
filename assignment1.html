Problem statement

Clothing-image recognition is a compact but meaningful example of visual perception. The ideal outcome of this assignment is not merely to obtain the highest test accuracy, but to understand how different neural-network families process the same visual evidence and to identify the trade-offs among predictive quality, computational cost, and inductive bias.

This is a predictive, multiclass single-label classification problem defined by the following core elements:

  • Prediction unit: One individual Fashion-MNIST image.
  • Input: A 28 × 28 grayscale clothing image.
  • Output: Ten real-valued logits, one per clothing class (T-shirt/top, Trouser, Pullover, Dress, Coat, Sandal, Shirt, Sneaker, Bag, Ankle boot).
  • Primary dataset: Fashion-MNIST.

By training and comparing these five architecture families under a common data split and evaluation protocol, observed differences can be attributed as fairly as possible to the model architecture and input representation.

Dataset description and EDA

Dataset description

Datasets, source and license

Two benchmarks from the MNIST family are involved. MNIST (handwritten digits) is used only while the pipeline is being built: checking DataLoader behavior, the forward and backward pass, and the evaluation metrics before any longer training run. It appears in none of the reported tables or figures. Fashion-MNIST is the primary dataset, and all five architectures (Linear/softmax, MLP, CNN, LSTM and Transformer) are trained, validated and tested on it with an identical split and evaluation protocol.

Fashion-MNIST was released in 2017 by Zalando Research (Han Xiao, Kashif Rasul and Roland Vollgraf) as a drop-in replacement for MNIST. It keeps the 28 × 28 grayscale format, the 10 classes and the 60,000 / 10,000 train/test split, but was designed as a more challenging benchmark. It is distributed under the MIT License (© 2017 Zalando SE) and hosted at github.com/zalandoresearch/fashion-mnist. We load the official release through torchvision.datasets.FashionMNIST.

Collection and labeling

The images are product photos from Zalando's online catalog. The original high-resolution color studio shots went through automatic background removal, aspect-ratio-preserving resizing, centering and downsampling to 28 × 28 grayscale. Each image carries exactly one of 10 labels, taken from Zalando's product category hierarchy. Our team did no manual relabeling or label cleaning.

Bias and limitations

  • Catalog bias: every image comes from studio photography with even lighting, centered framing and clean frontal or side views. Results on this benchmark say little about consumer photos with clutter, odd angles or uneven light.
  • Silhouette ambiguity: at 28 × 28, stitching, fabric weave, buttons and collars are mostly gone. T-shirt/top, Shirt, Pullover and Coat have very similar outlines, which is a built-in obstacle for linear and shallow models.
  • Artificial class balance: each class holds exactly 10.0% of the data, whereas real retail catalogs are skewed toward some categories. The upside is that a weak result on one class cannot be blamed on too few examples of it.

Class Distribution

EDA Figure 2 presents the class distribution after the canonical split. Stratification preserves exactly 4,800 training and 1,200 validation samples per class, so the standard deviation of class counts is zero within both development partitions. Since all classes are equally represented, the majority-class baseline accuracy is 10%. Because the dataset is balanced, overall accuracy provides a reliable summary of model performance. However, per-class F1 scores, Precision, Recall and confusion matrices are also reported to examine class-specific performance and identify common misclassification patterns. The confusion matrix is particularly useful for visually similar categories such as Shirt, T-shirt/top, Pullover, and Coat, as well as Sneaker and Ankle boot, because it shows which classes are most frequently confused with one another and how often these errors occur. This provides a clearer view of classification difficulties that may not be visible from overall accuracy alone.

Data Preprocessing

Intensity scaling and zero-centered standardization: Raw pixel values span [0, 255] as integers. As demonstrated in EDA Figure 3, about 50.2% of all pixels are zero (the black studio background), while approximately 49.8% form a broad foreground mass spanning 1 to 255. To eliminate numerical saturation and gradient instabilities, inputs are first scaled to [0.0, 1.0] via torchvision.transforms.ToTensor() (which also introduces the channel dimension to form a 1 × 28 × 28 tensor) and then standardized via channel-wise Z-score normalization:

x̃ = (x - μ) / σ   with   μ = 0.2863,   σ = 0.3532

Here, μ and σ are computed strictly on the 48,000-image training split, stabilizing gradient flow during backpropagation and speeding up convergence across all model backbones.

Asymmetric transformation pipeline: To balance regularization against deterministic evaluation integrity, the preprocessing pipeline is deliberately asymmetric between training and evaluation splits:

  • Training pipeline (train_transform):
    1. transforms.RandomRotation(degrees=(-10, 10)): Rotates the image randomly within ±10° to simulate minor natural hanging tilt and slight camera misalignment while keeping garments upright.
    2. transforms.RandomHorizontalFlip(p=0.5): Horizontally mirrors the image with 50% probability, exploiting the bilateral physical symmetry of most apparel and footwear (T-shirts, trousers, pullovers, sneakers) to double visual variety and enforce reflectional invariance.
    3. transforms.RandomCrop(size=28, padding=2): Pads the canvas by 2 zero-intensity pixels on all sides (matching the black studio background) and randomly crops back to 28 × 28, introducing subtle translation shifts of up to ±2 pixels to prevent models from overfitting to rigid center-pixel coordinate alignments.
    4. transforms.ToTensor(): Scales integer pixel intensities from [0, 255] to floating-point values in [0.0, 1.0] and reshapes the image into a channel-first tensor of shape (1, 28, 28).
    5. transforms.Normalize((0.2863,), (0.3532,)): Applies Z-score standardization computed strictly on the 48,000-image training split to center features around zero with approximately unit variance.
  • Evaluation pipeline (eval_transform): Validation is evaluated deterministically throughout training. After all development decisions and the checkpoint roster are fixed, the same deterministic pipeline is applied to each selected checkpoint for final evaluation on the official test set. Samples pass through transforms.ToTensor() followed by transforms.Normalize((0.2863,), (0.3532,)) using the training split's statistics.

Batching and architectural fairness: Preprocessed tensors are grouped by custom PyTorch DataLoader instances into mini-batches of batch_size = 128 for training (with random shuffle enabled) and batch_size = 256 for validation (without shuffle). The test DataLoader is constructed only in the final evaluation stage, also with batch_size = 256 and no shuffle. All five candidate models receive the exact same tensor batch format of shape (B, 1, 28, 28). Any structural transformations required by specific backbones — flattening into 784-dimensional vectors for Linear and MLP or patch projections for Transformer — are executed internally inside the respective model's forward pass. This guarantees that preprocessing remains a strictly controlled, invariant baseline across all architectural comparisons.

Comparison and discussion

Overall predictive performance

The CNN achieves the highest mean test Top-1 Accuracy (91.54 ± 0.20%), highest Macro-F1 (91.51 ± 0.19%), and lowest test cross-entropy loss (0.2301 ± 0.0030). Its mean accuracy exceeds the Linear baseline, MLP, LSTM, and Transformer by 16.77, 4.24, 0.69, and 1.11 percentage points, respectively. Macro-Recall equals accuracy because the test set contains the same number of images per class, so these two metrics do not provide independent evidence of improvement.

The MLP improves mean accuracy over Linear by 12.53 percentage points, although this comparison changes both nonlinearity and model capacity. LSTM ranks second in mean accuracy, exceeding Transformer by 0.42 percentage points. However, Transformer has a slightly lower mean test loss (0.2643 versus 0.2654), showing that accuracy and loss rankings are not identical.

Class-specific improvements and remaining errors

The CNN substantially improves recognition of difficult garment classes. Compared with Linear, its mean Shirt recall increases from 37.43% to 71.53%, while Shirt F1 increases from 39.64% to 73.56%. Mean Pullover-to-Coat errors decrease from 167.7 to 40.0, and Coat-to-Pullover errors decrease from 146.7 to 30.7. Nevertheless, Shirt remains the weakest class within every model, with T-shirt/top as its most frequent incorrect prediction.

The CNN does not lead every class-specific metric. LSTM achieves higher Dress recall than CNN (92.80% versus 91.57%) but lower precision (90.05% versus 91.27%), resulting in almost equal F1-scores. Transformer similarly achieves higher Shirt precision than CNN (78.64% versus 75.71%) but lower recall (69.10% versus 71.53%). These differences show why overall accuracy should be considered alongside class-specific precision, recall, and F1.

Architecture and input representation

The CNN's performance is consistent with the suitability of local receptive fields and shared convolutional filters for garment images. LSTM instead accumulates information through a sequence of image rows, while Transformer uses positional embeddings and self-attention to model relationships among patch tokens. These alternative representations also achieve strong predictive performance under the evaluated setup.

The nonlinear models have approximately comparable parameter counts, ranging from 230,730 to 242,762, whereas Linear contains only 7,850 parameters. However, the configurations also differ in depth, normalization, hidden dimensions, and dropout. The results therefore compare complete configurations and do not isolate the causal contribution of input representation or any individual architectural component.

Loss, training behavior, and prediction confidence

All models use cross-entropy loss, and each run is evaluated using the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss. Because cross-entropy depends on the probability assigned to the true class, its minimum need not coincide with maximum accuracy. For Transformer with seed 43, the selected checkpoint is epoch 81 with 91.29% validation accuracy, although epoch 86 reaches 91.47% with a higher validation loss.

The learning curves generally show rapid initial improvement followed by limited additional validation gains. LSTM shows a small late-stage separation between training and validation behavior, consistent with possible mild overfitting, without substantial sustained validation deterioration. Augmentation and training-mode regularization also affect these gaps. Qualitative examples include both correct predictions with small score margins and incorrect predictions with high softmax scores; CNN's lower test loss alone does not establish better calibration.

Accuracy, execution time, and parameter trade-offs

CNN improves mean accuracy over MLP by 4.24 percentage points while using 11,136 fewer parameters. LSTM offers a competitive alternative with 90.85 ± 0.10% accuracy: it trains for 65.67 ± 1.15 epochs and 1,270.6 ± 26.6 seconds per run, compared with CNN's 123.33 ± 6.43 epochs and 2,380.5 ± 213.7 seconds. Their mean epoch times are similar, so LSTM's shorter total training time mainly reflects fewer epochs under the shared early-stopping protocol.

Transformer has the highest mean epoch time (22.83 ± 0.33 seconds). Linear records the lowest mean inference time (0.1681 ± 0.0062 ms/sample), followed closely by MLP (0.1688 ± 0.0125 ms/sample). Training and inference measurements include data-pipeline operations and do not isolate model computation. Inference uses batches of 256, so time per sample does not represent single-image latency; the small timing differences should also be interpreted alongside variability across runs.

Hypothesis assessment and limitations

The results support the hypothesis that the evaluated nonlinear configurations outperform the linear classifier on the primary metric and the overall secondary predictive metrics. They also support the expectation that the CNN achieves the highest overall accuracy among the evaluated configurations. However, the class-specific exceptions show that this conclusion does not extend to every class and metric.

Confusion between visually similar classes

Description: Recurring errors involve similar upper-body garments and footwear categories. Shirt is the weakest class for all five models. CNN misclassifies 284.7 ± 12.1 Shirt images per run, corresponding to 28.47 ± 1.21%. Its most frequent incorrect predictions are T-shirt/top (110.7 ± 4.9 images) and Coat (85.0 ± 5.3 images). Compared with Linear, CNN reduces the mean Shirt-to-T-shirt/top error rate from 21.27% to 11.07% and improves Shirt recall from 37.43 ± 7.16% to 71.53 ± 1.21%.

For CNN, 40.0 ± 1.0 Pullover images are predicted as Coat (4.00 ± 0.10%), while 30.7 ± 2.9 Coat images are predicted as Pullover (3.07 ± 0.29%). The corresponding mean counts for Linear are 167.7 and 146.7. Transformer shows greater directional asymmetry, with 83.3 Coat images predicted as Pullover versus 31.7 Pullover images predicted as Coat on average.

Among footwear categories, CNN predicts 16.7 ± 3.1 Sneaker images as Ankle boot (1.67 ± 0.31%) and 37.7 ± 3.2 Ankle boot images as Sneaker (3.77 ± 0.32%). Another 24.7 ± 2.5 Sandal images are predicted as Sneaker (2.47 ± 0.25%).

Hypothesized cause: These errors may reflect similar overall shapes combined with poorly resolved distinguishing details. Upper-body garments can share sleeve shapes and body outlines, while footwear can share side profiles, soles, and upper contours. At 28 × 28 resolution, details such as necklines, collars, front openings, and ankle openings may be difficult to identify. Spatial downsampling could further reduce these details, although the experiments do not establish these factors as the causes of the observed errors.

Improvement ideas: Controlled ablations could evaluate reduced early downsampling or additional feature extraction before pooling. An augmentation ablation could also assess whether the current transformations help or hinder discrimination between similar classes. For Shirt specifically, targeted sampling of difficult training examples or moderate class weighting could address class difficulty despite the balanced dataset. Evaluation should monitor class-specific precision, recall, and both directions of each confusion pair, alongside parameter count and execution time.

Incorrect predictions with high softmax scores

Description: Selected MLP predictions from seed 42 include a Shirt image assigned 90.10% to Bag and only 5.57% to Shirt, and another Shirt image assigned 95.61% to T-shirt/top and 4.35% to Shirt. This group can overlap with confusion between visually similar classes. Its overall frequency has not been quantified, and selected examples alone do not establish systematic miscalibration.

Hypothesized cause: Learned visual features may strongly favor an incorrect category for a particular image. Cross-entropy training does not guarantee that a high softmax score corresponds to a correct prediction. The selected failures do not identify the specific features responsible.

Limitations and conclusion

Limitations

Data limitations

The study uses Fashion-MNIST images with a fixed resolution of 28 × 28 pixels and a single grayscale channel. Fine details such as collars, garment openings, and footwear structures can be difficult to distinguish at this resolution. The recurring confusion among similar upper-body garments is consistent with this limitation, although the experiments do not isolate the contribution of image resolution to these errors.

The balanced test set provides equal representation across classes, but does not evaluate performance under different class distributions. Furthermore, the dataset does not establish how the models would perform on photographs with different backgrounds, viewpoints, lighting conditions, or image acquisition procedures.

Method limitations

Each architecture is represented by one specific configuration. Although the nonlinear models have approximately comparable parameter counts, they differ in depth, normalization, dropout, and input representation. The comparisons therefore do not isolate the contribution of convolution, recurrence, self-attention, or individual regularization components.

The shared optimization and augmentation settings provide a common experimental protocol, but may not be equally suitable for every architecture. Consequently, the results may not establish the best achievable performance of each model family.

Training and inference times include data-pipeline operations and do not isolate model computation. Inference uses batches of 256, so the reported time per sample does not represent single-image latency. Timing results depend on the reported hardware and execution settings.

Generalization and future work

The held-out test results demonstrate generalization to unseen Fashion-MNIST images under the evaluated protocol. Further experiments could use additional training seeds, alternative train/validation partitions, controlled architectural and regularization ablations, and validation-based hyperparameter tuning. Shirt recognition and confusion among similar classes remain priorities. Confidence calibration, separate computation benchmarks, and evaluation under distribution shifts would provide a broader assessment.

Conclusion

Under the shared evaluation protocol, the CNN is the most effective configuration for Fashion-MNIST classification. It achieves the highest mean test top-1 accuracy of 91.54 ± 0.20%, the highest mean Macro-F1 of 91.51 ± 0.19%, and the lowest mean test cross-entropy loss of 0.2301 ± 0.0030. Its mean accuracy exceeds the linear classifier by 16.77 percentage points.

The results support the hypothesis that the evaluated nonlinear configurations outperform the linear classifier on the primary metric and the overall secondary predictive metrics. They also support the expectation that the CNN achieves the highest overall accuracy among the evaluated configurations. However, the class-specific exceptions show that this conclusion does not extend to every class and metric.

The results support the suitability of the CNN configuration for this task, while highlighting persistent confusion between Shirt and visually similar garment classes as a priority for further improvement.
